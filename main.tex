\title{A VR Digital Twin for UAV Teleoperation under Degraded Video Links: Geospatial Priors and Semantic Object-State Telemetry in Unreal Engine}{VR Digital Twin for UAV Teleoperation}

\author[1]{Xabier OLAZ}{}

\abstract{Virtual-reality headsets and intelligent hardware enable immersive operator interfaces for unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) teleoperation. Under variable link conditions---delay, reduced bandwidth, intermittent connectivity---the pilot can lose spatial awareness of terrain, obstacles, and mission objects. We present a VR-headset digital-twin pipeline in which an Unreal Engine/Cesium geospatial prior provides static scene context and an onboard detector's semantic object-state updates are reconstructed as time-stamped, uncertainty-aware actors in the operator's headset. The headset is not a passive monitor: stereoscopic depth supports distance judgments that degraded video erodes first, and head-tracked rendering decouples the operator's gaze from the camera gimbal, so the space around the aircraft stays inspectable. We validate the chain on a real-flight replay: recorded UAV video replayed over a simulated per-frame link with synchronized ArduPilot telemetry, a fine-tuned detector (mAP50 0.98), and objects reconstructed as geometric proxy actors. The twin reproduced the logged trajectory within 0.24 m; the reconstructed power-line support fell 4.3 m from orthophoto ground truth. Semantic telemetry carried the mission at 615.8 kbps as emitted (1.67 Mbps normalized), 84.0\% and 70.4\% below H.264 and H.265 encodings of the same video. Detection-to-ingestion latency averaged 38.2 ms, with end-to-end updates bounded at 188 ms; the OpenXR stereoscopic rendering path, measured under Quest 2/3 display profiles, costs 15--23 ms GPU frame time, within headset budgets. The display is an auxiliary aid for pilot spatial awareness, not certified detect-and-avoid or a replacement for regulatory safety channels.}

\section{Introduction}

Remote UAV operation is a spatial decision-making problem. A pilot or mission operator must reason about aircraft pose, terrain, hazards, people, vehicles, mission targets, visibility, and communication degradation while often relying on a video stream and a map display. Modern video links can be effective under favorable coverage, bandwidth, latency, and codec conditions; this paper does not assume that video is intrinsically inadequate. The problem addressed here is narrower: under variable link conditions (delayed, reduced, or intermittently connected operating states), continuous high-quality low-latency video may be insufficient for reasoning about objects outside the camera's current field of view. 

The operational setting makes the display question concrete. BVLOS inspection pilots increasingly work from a portable ground station: a laptop in the field, outdoors, often in direct sunlight, splitting attention between a video window, a 2D map, and telemetry widgets. That setting imposes three display constraints that are independent of the link. First, readability: laptop panels are glare-limited outdoors, while a head-mounted display offers a constant-contrast, private, effectively unbounded canvas. Second, attention geometry: the operator alternates gaze between screen, controller, and sky; an immersive scene keeps the mission world inside a single egocentric frame instead of distributing it across windows. Third, and decisive, field of regard: a video feed shows only what the camera currently points at, at the camera's own orientation, whereas the operator's need is to reason about what is around, behind, or below the aircraft. A reconstructed scene can be inspected off-axis by turning one's head; a video cannot.

Why a VR headset specifically, rather than a better flat render of the same scene? Because three properties of the medium map one-to-one onto the operator's tasks. Stereoscopic depth is the natural channel for judging relative distance to obstacles, terrain, and the aircraft's own altitude trend---exactly the judgments a compressed 2D video degrades first. Head-tracked rendering decouples the operator's gaze from the camera gimbal, so the twin can be examined from any viewpoint while the aircraft keeps flying its mission. And a persistent 3D frame lets map-derived, live, stale, and uncertain entities coexist as spatially distinct encodings (Section~\ref{sec:scene-state}), supporting the perception--comprehension--projection chain of situation awareness \cite{endsley1995theory} in a single glance sequence. In short, the HMD is not a nicer monitor for the same pixels: it is the only display class whose properties directly compensate the specific information losses of a degraded video link.

The central problem is the pilot's need to maintain situation awareness from partial, delayed, heterogeneous, and differently trustworthy evidence. A geospatial map or 3D-tile layer can preserve useful static context, but it does not by itself encode what has changed, what is currently observed, what is stale, or what is uncertain. The scientific gap addressed here is the operator-facing interface for combining a geospatial prior with live semantic state updates: the prior supplies static operating context, while dynamic detections or sensor-derived events update only the entities that are new, moving, changed, uncertain, or absent from the map. The validation burden is to show that this dynamic, uncertainty-aware VR scene supports spatial awareness rather than simply producing an attractive visualization.

Virtual reality and telepresence research provide the conceptual basis for this operator-display problem. Virtual reality can be understood through the dimensions that determine telepresence \cite{steuer1992vr}, while situation awareness includes perception of relevant elements, comprehension of their meaning, and projection of their future status \cite{endsley1995theory}. 

The proposed VR-Headset Visual Pipeline addresses this problem by combining a geospatial prior with live semantic-state updates. The architecture connects the onboard detector to a ground-station service that normalizes and publishes a current state snapshot. Unreal Engine then renders the resulting actors over Cesium geospatial context for an operator wearing a VR headset. The reconstructed scene is an auxiliary operator display in which map-derived context, observed dynamic actors, stale actors, and unobserved space must remain visually and semantically distinguishable.

The main research question is: \textit{Can a VR-equipped UAV operator maintain better spatial awareness of mission-relevant terrain, objects, and changes under constrained communication than with video-only or non-immersive hybrid displays, while preserving explicit visibility of map-derived, observed, stale, or uncertain information?}

The intended contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Defining a VR-headset-centered operator task model for UAV teleoperation, where a geospatial prior is used to preserve static spatial context, while semantic state updates dynamically represent newly detected or changing entities.
    \item Proposing a VR digital-twin architecture that integrates the VR headset, Unreal Engine, Cesium geospatial prior, and a ground-station service with the onboard detector to combine static maps with live semantic scene reconstruction.
    \item A scene-state encoding designed for the VR medium rather than ported to it: map-derived, live, stale, and uncertain entities are distinct stereoscopic layers whose meaning is read through depth and head-tracked inspection (Section~\ref{sec:scene-state}). To our knowledge this is the first UAV operator display in which the HMD is the primary consumer of a semantic telemetry stream, making freshness and uncertainty spatially inspectable properties of the scene---a class of immersive display that a video feed cannot express regardless of its bitrate.
    \item Validating the complete chain through a real-flight replay, which provides measured semantic bandwidth reductions against H.264 and H.265 baselines, alongside sub-metre trajectory fidelity and orthophoto-referenced geospatial accuracy for reconstructed objects.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work and Positioning}

The VR-Headset Visual Pipeline sits at the intersection of immersive teleoperation, digital twins, semantic communication, and geospatial visualization. 

\subsection{Immersive Teleoperation and Digital Twins}
Immersive interfaces have been studied to improve operator perception in unmanned-vehicle control \cite{fabris2021immersive, roldan2017multirobot}. Recent work on VR teleoperation has explored visual augmentation of live streams \cite{luo2024visual} and compared 3D visualization and VR display designs for remote monitoring \cite{buchner2025remote}. UAV-specific VR and digital-twin studies include augmented-reality mobile digital twins for wildfire prevention \cite{costa2025ardigitaltwin} and VR-based teleoperation for UAV-manipulator systems \cite{yang2025uavmanipulator}. VRIH has explicitly highlighted the connection between virtual reality, intelligent hardware, and digital twins \cite{lv2022vrihdt, ahmed2022vrihdt}. Broader surveys characterize digital twins as cyber-physical representations requiring data synchronization \cite{jones2020dt, barricelli2019dt, singh2021dt}. Our pipeline differs from these works by focusing on a layered prior-plus-state-update operator scene rather than only viewpoint control or simulated piloting.

\subsection{Geospatial Priors and Semantic Communication}
The geospatial-prior layer is grounded in 3D geospatial visualization practice. The OGC 3D Tiles specification defines an open standard for streaming massive 3D geospatial datasets \cite{ogc2022threedtiles}, making it plausible to use Cesium/Unreal as a static scene prior. To update this prior, instead of transmitting full video, the pipeline sends semantic telemetry: the detected entities and their display-relevant attributes. While this differs from theoretical deep-learning semantic communication systems \cite{luo2022semantic, xie2021semantic, shao2022edgeinference}, it offers an operational reduction in bandwidth by transmitting only task-relevant information.

\subsection{UAV Perception and Variable Link Conditions}
UAV object detection is treated as an upstream perception component \cite{cao2023uavdetection}. Vision-based UAV target geolocation has been extensively studied \cite{barber2006geolocation, hosseinpoor2016geolocation, liu2024multiview}. While modern video links (LTE, WebRTC, 5G) can achieve stable low-latency transmissions under ideal conditions \cite{mohamed2025lteuav, chodorek2025webrtc, duangsuwan2026uocrf}, they still suffer from coverage-capacity trade-offs, handovers, and bandwidth constraints in rural or urban air mobility scenarios \cite{chen2025uamstreaming, chintareddy2026vertical}. The relevant scenario for our visual pipeline is an operator task in which video is available only intermittently or at reduced resolution. While multi-sensor fusion (IR, LiDAR, radar) can improve perception under low visibility \cite{li2025irvisuav, farina2026lidarradar, fercho2026faa_svs}, our current implementation uses YOLO on RGB, integrating other sensors as future work.

\section{System Overview}

\subsection{Operator Scenario}
The target scenario is a UAV mission in which a remote operator needs to monitor terrain, aircraft state, obstacles, or mission objects while communication quality is constrained. The operator wears a VR headset and inspects a synthetic 3D scene containing a geospatial prior, UAV pose, detected entities, freshness indicators, and uncertainty indicators. The proposed display is auxiliary and should be used alongside regulatory control links and operational procedures. Figure~\ref{fig:system-overview} shows the complete system with the real validation assets: a one-way semantic downlink, an independent command-and-control uplink, the ground-station reconstruction, and the operator's head-tracked stereo view.

\subsection{Architecture}
The VR-Headset Visual Pipeline is organized around the separation between static scene context and dynamic semantic updates, arranged in the four operating zones of Figure~\ref{fig:system-overview} (A: real UAV, B: link, C: ground backend, D: digital twin and pilot). At initialization, Unreal Engine and Cesium build the map-derived geospatial prior over the designated operating area, while the onboard detection source concurrently publishes dynamic objects to a ground-station ingestion service. This service normalizes their source identity, class, confidence, timestamp, uncertainty, and position into a consistent operator-display representation, and exposes the resulting state as a snapshot that a dedicated telemetry component inside Unreal polls at 5~Hz. On each poll, Unreal reconciles the received entities with the live scene by spawning, updating, marking stale, or despawning semantic actors, so that the VR-equipped operator ultimately perceives the fused geospatial prior and the reconstructed semantic-state layer as a single immersive display.

In the intended live deployment, the UAV streams semantic telemetry and autopilot pose data over a wireless link to the ground station, allowing the pilot to navigate remotely using the VR headset and the reconstructed Unreal/Cesium digital twin without relying on a continuous direct video feed. The validation reported in this paper, however, is a recorded-flight replay over a simulated local link rather than a live field trial.

\subsection{Implementation Workflow}
The runtime distinguishes a one-time static-context setup from a continuous dynamic-update loop, following the four operating zones of Figure~\ref{fig:system-overview}. Unreal and Cesium initialize the map-derived scene prior once, after which the system enters a publish--normalize--poll cycle: the detection source emits each new observation; the ground-station service normalizes source identity, class, confidence, timestamp, uncertainty, and position into a canonical entity record; and the Unreal telemetry component polls that snapshot at 5~Hz to reconcile the state with the live scene. On every poll, the component spawns newly confirmed actors, updates the pose of tracked ones, demotes those whose age exceeds the configured staleness threshold, and despawns actors whose tracks have expired. This design keeps the heavy geospatial prior on the ground station while the downlink to the operator carries only the semantic delta, which is the basis of the bandwidth advantage reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Data Representation and Actor Lifecycle}
Each reconstructed object is represented as a display entity:
\begin{equation}
e_i(t) = \{ id_i, c_i, q_i, p_i, \Sigma_i, s_i, a_i, g_i, r_i, m_i \},
\end{equation}
where \(id_i\) is identity, \(c_i\) class, \(q_i\) confidence, \(p_i\) position, \(\Sigma_i\) uncertainty, \(s_i\) source, \(a_i\) age, \(g_i\) geometry, \(r_i\) information role (prior, live, stale), and \(m_i\) metadata. 

The display actor lifecycle is: \text{new} $\rightarrow$ \text{confirmed} $\rightarrow$ \text{updated} $\rightarrow$ \text{stale} $\rightarrow$ \text{removed}. An entity is marked stale when $a_i(t) = t - t_i > \tau_{\mathrm{stale}}$. The replay configuration implements track TTLs of 30~s (static) and 4~s (dynamic).

\subsection{Network Interface and Message Contract}
\label{sec:message-contract}
Unreal does not ingest the raw autopilot stream or the raw detector output. A ground-station service normalizes these two asynchronous sources into a single polled snapshot. The detector publishes detections over a local ingestion interface; the autopilot supplies aircraft state over its telemetry link. The service fuses both, resolves a stable per-object identity, and exposes the operator-display state as a snapshot that the Unreal telemetry component polls at 5~Hz. Each snapshot carries two top-level blocks: a \code{telemetry} object for the UAV and an \code{obstacles[]} array for the reconstructed entities. The position is carried both as WGS84 latitude/longitude and as a local ENU \code{world\_m} triple relative to a recorded home origin, and the Unreal component prioritizes \code{world\_m} when present.

The \code{telemetry} block carries the geodetic position of the aircraft (\code{lat}, \code{lon}, \code{alt}, \code{rel\_alt}), its local ENU position relative to home (\code{world\_m.\{north,east,up\}}, preferred by Unreal), the aircraft heading used for actor orientation (\code{yaw}, \code{heading}), and flight-mode, arm-state, and ground-speed context (\code{mode}, \code{armed}, \code{groundspeed}). Each entry of \code{obstacles[]} carries a stable identity for the spawn/update/despawn lifecycle (\code{entity\_id}, \code{object\_id}), a canonical class label such as \textit{tower}, \textit{cow}, or \textit{bike} (\code{type}, \code{object\_type}), the local ENU position with geodetic fallback (\code{world\_m}, \code{lat}/\code{lon}), the detection confidence that drives confirmed/stale demotion, an optional object heading (\code{yaw\_deg}, \code{heading\_deg}), and track freshness and static/dynamic metadata used for TTL selection (\code{track\_age\_s}, \code{track\_seen\_count}, \code{track\_static}).

\section{Method}

\subsection{Layered Scene-State Model}
\label{sec:scene-state}
At time \(t\), the operator display is modeled as a layered scene state:
\begin{equation}
S(t) = \{G_0(\Omega), x_u(t), E_{\Delta}(t), U(t), H(t)\},
\end{equation}
where \(G_0(\Omega)\) is the geospatial prior, \(x_u(t)\) is UAV state, \(E_{\Delta}(t)\) is the set of semantic updates, \(U(t)\) contains uncertainty/freshness annotations, and \(H(t)\) denotes the VR-headset view. This model allows the system to remain useful when live video is degraded: the display is not blank because \(G_0(\Omega)\), \(x_u(t)\), and the last validated \(E_{\Delta}(t)\) provide bounded spatial context.

\subsection{Scene-State Visual Encoding in the HMD}
\label{sec:visual-encoding}
The layered state of Section~\ref{sec:scene-state} is only useful if the operator can read evidence quality at a glance, so each layer is assigned a distinct visual channel rather than a distinct menu or window (Figure~\ref{fig:system-overview}, zone~D). The geospatial prior \(G_0\) renders as the muted photorealistic Cesium base---recognizable but visually subordinate. Live entities (information role \textit{live}) render as saturated proxy actors. Stale entities are demoted, not deleted: on crossing the staleness threshold \(\tau_{\mathrm{stale}}\) (30~s for static tracks, 4~s for dynamic ones in the replay configuration) the actor loses saturation and acquires an age annotation, so that aged evidence remains visible but can no longer masquerade as current; on track expiry the actor despawns. Positional uncertainty \(\Sigma_i\) is rendered as an explicit bounded volume around the entity rather than hidden inside a point estimate, and confidence gates entry into the scene in the first place. Two properties make this encoding VR-specific rather than a transplanted HUD convention. First, demotion and uncertainty are read through stereoscopic depth and head-tracked inspection---the operator assesses \emph{where} the evidence is weak in the same 3D frame used for the mission, without diverting gaze to a side panel. Second, the encoding is the only channel through which the freshness of a semantic stream becomes spatially inspectable: a video feed can degrade, but it cannot display the provenance and age of the information it carries. The encoding rules are enforced by the actor lifecycle and bounded by the safety envelope (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), which exists precisely to prevent a clean, confident-looking render from overstating the evidence behind it.

\subsection{Detection-to-World Projection}
\label{sec:detection-to-world}
The georeferencing step converts one bounding box into one world position under explicit camera, pose, and terrain assumptions (Figure~\ref{fig:system-overview}, zone~A detail). The detector returns a box \(b=[x_1,y_1,x_2,y_2]^T\); because the objects of interest rest on the ground, the contact evidence is the bottom-centre pixel of the box,
\begin{equation}
z_b = \begin{bmatrix} u_b \\ v_b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (x_1+x_2)/2 \\ y_2 \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
which is the pixel least sensitive to box width noise and to the object's visible extent above the ground plane. Under a pinhole model with image size \(W\times H\) and calibrated vertical field of view \(\psi_v\), \(z_b\) maps to a unit ray in the camera frame; the ray is then rotated into the UAV body frame using the known camera mount, and into local North--East--Down (NED) using the attitude (yaw, pitch, roll) logged by the autopilot. The mount and attitude terms are what place the ray off-nadir in the zone~A detail of Figure~\ref{fig:system-overview}: the estimate never assumes the camera points straight down. With NED down positive and the ray \(\hat r_n=[r_{n,N}, r_{n,E}, r_{n,D}]^T\), intersection with the local ground plane at UAV height \(h\) (AGL) gives
\begin{equation}
t_b = \frac{h}{r_{n,D}}, \quad N=t_b r_{n,N}, \quad E=t_b r_{n,E}, \quad d_h=\sqrt{N^2+E^2},
\end{equation}
so the world position is the aircraft position translated by \((N,E)\) in the local tangent frame, published to the twin as \code{world\_m}. Four assumptions bound the estimate: a calibrated FOV and mount, flat local terrain, tight video--telemetry timestamp alignment, and a ground-contact object. The dominant error source is attitude noise scaling with \(t_b\): the longer the slant range, the more a fraction of a degree moves the ground point, which is why off-nadir geometry---not detector accuracy---sets the error floor. The measured end-to-end error of the complete chain (detector, projection, synchronization, and twin placement) is 4.3~m against orthophoto ground truth for the validated support (Section~\ref{sec:trajectory-fidelity}), and the replayed aircraft trajectory is reproduced within 0.24~m; the projection is therefore used as an estimate with a measured error envelope, not as a proof of target location.

\subsection{Semantic Telemetry Bandwidth}
For a mission interval \(T\), with \(N\) entity messages of size \(|m_k|\) bytes, semantic telemetry bitrate is:
\begin{equation}
B_{\mathrm{sem}} = \frac{8}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{N} |m_k|.
\end{equation}
The total display traffic is \(B_{\mathrm{display}} = B_{\mathrm{pose}} + B_{\Delta} + B_{\mathrm{prior}}\). If the operating area is pre-cached, \(B_{\mathrm{prior}}\) is near zero. The bandwidth reduction against a video baseline is \(\Delta B = (B_{\mathrm{video}} - B_{\mathrm{sem}}) / B_{\mathrm{video}}\).

\subsection{Latency Decomposition}
End-to-end latency from source event to visible update in the VR headset is:
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{e2e}} = L_{\mathrm{det}} + L_{\mathrm{uplink}} + L_{\mathrm{svc}} + L_{\mathrm{poll}} + L_{\mathrm{actor}} + L_{\mathrm{render}} + L_{\mathrm{vr}}.
\end{equation}
The software-only segments can be instrumented immediately. The complete source-to-VR-headset measurement requires the Unreal VR runtime and the operator's VR headset.

\section{Results: Real-Flight Replay Validation}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Replay Setup and Synchronization}
The validation uses a recorded power-line inspection flight (M\_20\_1RR) operated by FuVeX Civil S.L. The complete onboard video (69.2~s) was replayed frame by frame over HTTP through a simulated per-frame link, with ArduPilot telemetry synchronized to the video by content matching against the recorded frames. A 23.9~s analysis segment (1280$\times$960, 10~fps) covering the closest pass over the inspected supports drives the synchronized twin comparison of Section~\ref{sec:twin-sync}. A tower detector fine-tuned from a YOLO11n base reaches mAP50 0.98 on the portrait validation split. Detected supports are georeferenced, tracked by the ground-station service, and reconstructed as proxy actors. The detection output and its synchronized reconstruction in the twin are shown side by side in Figure~\ref{fig:twin-sync-validation}.

\subsection{Semantic Bandwidth versus Video Baselines}
Over the replayed mission, the detector published 554 obstacle messages totalling 14.46~MB. Two rates characterize the stream (Figure~\ref{fig:bandwidth}): 615.8~kbps as emitted over the 187.9~s wall-clock replay window, and 1.67~Mbps when normalized to the 69.2~s mission interval. Baselines were encoded from the same flight video: H.264 (CRF~28) at 10.43~Mbps and H.265 (CRF~30) at 5.65~Mbps. The mission-normalized reduction is 84.0\% against H.264 and 70.4\% against H.265. These figures exclude Cesium tile traffic, as the operating region was pre-cached.

\subsection{Latency}
The replay metrics report the detection-to-ingestion latency---frame timestamp to acceptance by the ground-station service---for 649 latency samples carried within the 554 obstacle messages: mean 38.2~ms, p95 252.9~ms (Figure~\ref{fig:latency-hist}). Adding a conservative display budget for the remaining software segments (5~Hz poll and 50~ms actor spawn/render) bounds the end-to-end detection-to-display update latency at about 188~ms mean for a visible proxy update in the desktop twin. The stereoscopic rendering cost that completes the path to the headset is measured separately in Section~\ref{sec:hmd-budget}.

\subsection{Trajectory Fidelity and Geospatial Accuracy}
\label{sec:trajectory-fidelity}
The twin aircraft was driven by the service-published world positions. Against the reference trajectory, the commanded path shows a cross-track error of 0.24~m maximum ($n=2363$). Ground truth for power-line supports was marked on PNOA orthophotos. A robust cluster analysis places the main cluster (216 observations, support P3) at 4.3~m from its orthophoto position; the end-to-end georeferencing error is approximately 4--5~m for the validated support at 40--130~m range. Residual error sources: camera-mount residual (2--8~m) and video--log synchronization (8--12~m along track).

\subsection{Synchronized Twin Correspondence}
\label{sec:twin-sync}
As a visual end-to-end check, the onboard video and the twin were replayed in sync and captured side by side over the full analysis segment. Figure~\ref{fig:twin-sync-validation} shows two instants: when the detector confirms a support, the semantic telemetry stream spawns the proxy actor at the georeferenced ENU pose and it persists for the rest of the flight. The correspondence claim is event-level and geospatial (spawn on confirmed detection, proxy at the measured ENU pose with the errors reported in Section~\ref{sec:trajectory-fidelity}); the twin render is driven by the reconstructed state, not by pixel matching against the onboard imagery, so viewpoint and terrain appearance differ from the real frames.

\subsection{Desktop Render Performance}
\label{sec:render-performance}
To bound the frame cost of the reconstructed semantic actors, we benchmarked the packaged semantic-proxy scene with the same engine on the workstation listed in Table~\ref{tab:implementation-config} (AMD Ryzen~9 9950X, RTX~5090, 64~GB RAM). The benchmark replayed synthetic obstacle payloads through the same ingestion path used at runtime and reported per-phase frame time together with estimated component, triangle, and draw-call counts; the benchmark scene contains the proxy actors and their update streams but not the Cesium tileset, whose streaming cost is measured in Section~\ref{sec:hmd-budget}.

Across the measured scene sizes (10, 50, and 100 reconstructed semantic-proxy actors), the create, pose-stream, and shape-stream phases stayed within a 33.3~ms (30~FPS) frame-time budget, with per-phase frame times ranging from about 4.4~ms at 10 actors to about 19.3~ms for the heaviest 100-actor shape-update stream, and scene complexity growing to 432 components / 432 draw calls and roughly 209k triangles at 100 actors. On the lighter global workload, the Unreal CSV profiler reported a global \code{GPUTime} median of 1.46~ms and \code{FrameTime} median of 4.66~ms. The 100-object pose- and shape-update streams exceed a 16.7~ms (60~FPS) budget, which bounds the dense-scene update rate but leaves single-frame rendering headroom on this hardware. These measurements are non-stereoscopic desktop measurements without the geospatial tileset; they establish a smooth desktop baseline for the dynamic-actor load and are complemented by the stereoscopic-path measurements of Section~\ref{sec:hmd-budget}, which include the Cesium prior.

\subsection{Stereoscopic Rendering Path and HMD Frame Budget}
\label{sec:hmd-budget}
The immersive display is served by the same Unreal scene through the engine's OpenXR stereoscopic path: one scene update produces both eye views, so the semantic-actor lifecycle measured above is shared between eyes and the only additional cost over the desktop baseline is the second view's rasterization and the compositor stage. We measured this path directly: the replayed flight scene was run in VR mode against the Meta XR Simulator v81 OpenXR runtime with the exact display profiles of the Meta Quest~3 (2064$\times$2208 per eye) and Meta Quest~2 (1832$\times$1920 per eye), capturing the engine CSV profiler over complete flight replays, with a monoscopic run of the same scene as the control. Statistics are computed over steady flight, discarding the initial Cesium tile-streaming warm-up that dominates the first seconds of each capture.

The stereoscopic GPU frame cost during the flight measured 15.0~ms median (p90 16.8~ms, max 20.1~ms) under the Quest~3 display profile and 23.4~ms median (p90 27.5~ms) under the Quest~2 profile, against 4--5~ms (p90) for the monoscopic control of the same scene: in like-for-like p90 terms the stereoscopic path multiplies the GPU frame cost by about 3.7 (16.8~ms vs 4.6~ms), consistent with the second eye view plus the compositor, and remains within the 60~FPS GPU budget on the Quest~3 profile and within the 30~FPS budget on the Quest~2 profile. The CPU game-thread cost was identical in the monoscopic and stereoscopic runs (it is dominated by geospatial tile streaming during camera motion, not by rendering), so the stereoscopic path adds no CPU bottleneck: the only VR-specific cost is the measured GPU increment. These measurements capture the application-side stereoscopic rendering and compositor path with exact device display profiles; the runtime executes on the workstation GPU, so they characterize the scene's stereo rendering cost and its budget headroom rather than the Quest mobile SoC, whose on-device budget is further eased by fixed-foveated rendering and a lower per-eye pixel count than the desktop window used here.

\subsection{Degradation Profiles}
Network degradation was simulated using loss and jitter profiles: visible-entity recall was 0.949 under 5\% loss with 50~ms jitter and 0.843 under 15\% loss with 200~ms jitter. The actor lifecycle behaved as specified: proxy actors spawn on detection, persist through tracked updates, are demoted when stale, and despawn on track expiry.

\subsection{HMD Recognition Feasibility}
\label{sec:hmd-feasibility}
As an end-to-end feasibility check, one operator---a member of the research team---wearing a Meta Quest~2 headset (Table~\ref{tab:implementation-config}) inspected the reconstructed scene during the real-flight replay and identified the reconstructed semantic actors of the three canonical classes used in the workflow---power-line supports (\textit{tower}), livestock (\textit{cow}), and vehicles/bicycles (\textit{bike})---from the semantic state stream alone, with the video feed disabled. The check closes the motivational loop of the pipeline. The link budget of Section~\ref{sec:results} is the reason the system exists: the semantic stream sustains mission state at 615.8~kbps as emitted---about 6\% of the H.264 encoding of the same flight---a budget under which flying from continuous real-time video is the first capability to fail, while per-object state updates still get through. The headset then turns that surviving stream into perception: stereoscopic depth restores the perspective and distance cues that degraded video suppresses first, and head-tracked inspection lets the operator localize the aircraft, the terrain, and mission objects relative to each other---the spatial component of situation awareness \cite{endsley1995theory}---rather than relative to a flat video window. That a single operator could read the canonical classes from the state stream alone indicates that the reconstructed scene carries its intended meaning in the medium it was designed for. Quantifying the operational benefit---task performance, NASA-TLX workload, and SAGAT awareness scores against video-only, prior-only, and non-immersive baselines---requires the controlled operator study defined as future work (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Limitations and Safety Envelope}
\label{sec:limitations}

The VR-Headset Visual Pipeline can mislead an operator if uncertainty is hidden, and several limitations are inherent to the system definition. On the semantic side, an absent actor may denote an undetected object rather than safe empty space, and a stale actor can retain an authoritative appearance unless its age, source, and confidence are visibly demoted in the display. On the geospatial side, the prior can be outdated, omit temporary obstacles such as cables, and accumulate placement errors whenever terrain or coordinate systems are misaligned; the measured 4--5~m error therefore bounds the display to spatial-awareness support rather than precision maneuvering.

The communication advantage itself rests on a pre-cached operating region, since streaming geospatial tiles over the same constrained link would erode the bandwidth reduction. The immersive interface additionally inherits the human-factor risks common to VR, including cybersickness, attentional tunneling, fatigue, and display occlusion. From a regulatory standpoint, Beyond Visual Line of Sight operation requires certified detect-and-avoid and command-and-control reliability that an auxiliary display of this kind cannot provide.

The validation reported here is deliberately bounded: it covers a single flight, a single scenario, a single object class, and a simulated local link, so generalization will require additional flights and real radio measurements. Real-time onboard performance is likewise not established; the separate non-stereoscopic desktop render benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:render-performance}) covers the semantic-actor update load without the Cesium tileset, while the stereoscopic path is measured through OpenXR device profiles (Section~\ref{sec:hmd-budget}) on the full geospatial scene, whose workstation-backed runtime characterizes the scene's stereo rendering cost rather than the Quest mobile SoC.

The safety envelope operationalizes these constraints through bounded stale-object age (a track TTL of 30~s for static entities and 4~s for dynamic ones), a maximum position-uncertainty radius, a minimum detection confidence, fallback rules that visually demote actors on staleness and despawn them on expiry, and an explicit prohibition against using the display as a certified detect-and-avoid channel.

\section{Discussion and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}

The proposed system's effectiveness depends on several validity requirements. First, spatial awareness must be measured through task performance and established human-factor instruments (such as NASA-TLX or SAGAT) rather than inferred from visual plausibility. Second, baseline parity between video-only, prior-only, non-immersive hybrid, and VR digital-twin conditions is essential. Third, the VR-headset scene must be strictly tied to logs, timestamps, and ground truth.

This structure makes the core hypothesis falsifiable. The pipeline is supported only if the VR digital-twin condition improves or preserves task-relevant spatial awareness under constrained communication without unacceptable increases in workload or overtrust. The strongest version of the contribution is that a VR operator display can explicitly combine prior scene knowledge and live semantic state updates in a way that remains inspectable under degraded video.

\textbf{Future Work:} The immediate next steps include a hardware-in-the-loop campaign with a live autopilot, field measurements over a real degraded radio link, completion of the corridor ground-truth map, on-device packaging for the Quest mobile runtime, and a controlled operator study to measure human utility and workload.

\section{Conclusion}

The VR-Headset Visual Pipeline is presented as a human-in-the-loop digital-twin operator display for UAV teleoperation. The implemented workflow uses Unreal/Cesium as a bounded geospatial prior, converts detected changes into semantic telemetry, publishes a live entity snapshot through the ground-station service, and reconstructs dynamic actors inside a VR-headset-visible scene. The publishable contribution is that the integrated VR-headset scene layer can be specified, instrumented, and evaluated as an operator aid for spatial awareness when video-only displays may be insufficient. The stored-flight replay validation confirms content-synchronized video and telemetry, detection at mAP50 0.98, semantic telemetry at 1.67~Mbps mission-normalized (84.0\% and 70.4\% below H.264 and H.265), detection-to-ingestion latency of 38.2~ms mean, sub-metre trajectory fidelity, a 4.3~m geospatial error against orthophoto ground truth, and a measured OpenXR stereoscopic GPU frame cost of 15--23~ms under the Quest~3 and Quest~2 display profiles.

The role of virtual reality in this system is constitutive, not cosmetic. The pipeline converts a bandwidth-starved detection stream into a world the operator can step into: stereoscopic depth recovers the relative-distance cues that degraded video suppresses, head-tracked rendering frees inspection from the camera's field of view, and the layered scene-state encoding makes map-derived, live, stale, and uncertain evidence spatially distinguishable in a single immersive frame. The headset is thus the component that turns semantic telemetry from a compression result into an operational capability. Establishing the magnitude of that capability---a controlled operator study against video-only, prior-only, and non-immersive baselines, with validated latency and workload instruments---is the direct continuation of this work.
